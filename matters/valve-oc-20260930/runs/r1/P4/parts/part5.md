### Description

#### Manufacture of the seat ring

In the illustrated embodiment the seat ring (30) is made from tube stock of a superelastic alloy, NiTi of nominally 55.8 wt% nickel content, having an outer diameter of 2.7 mm and a wall of 0.20 mm. As shown in FIG. 8A, the tube stock is mounted on a mandrel and the profile of the seat ring is cut from it by laser cutting. The convoluted wall (32) may be defined by the laser-cut profile, by forming on the mandrel, by the shape-setting fixture described below, or by a combination of these steps; the disclosure of the convoluted wall does not depend on which of these steps imparts the convolution, provided that the finished seat ring has the convoluted wall and the sealing edge (34) described above.

As shown in FIG. 8B, the cut seat ring (30) is then subjected to shape-setting: it is held in a fixture that constrains the convoluted wall (32) to its intended wave geometry and is heated, in the illustrated embodiment at 500 °C for 10 minutes, and then water-quenched. Shape-setting fixes the at-rest geometry of the seat ring, which is the geometry to which the seat ring returns by shape recovery when the pressure differential falls. Shape-setting time and temperature, together with the nickel content, also determine the transformation temperatures of the superelastic alloy. In the illustrated embodiment the seat ring is specified to have an austenite finish temperature below 5 °C, so that it is fully austenitic and behaves superelastically at ordinary riding temperatures. The values of 500 °C and 10 minutes are those used for the prototype seat rings of Example 1; in some embodiments the shape-setting temperature and time are selected so as to hold the austenite finish temperature of production seat rings below the specified value, and in some embodiments the austenite finish temperature is verified on sampled seat rings after shape-setting.

After shape-setting, the seat ring (30) is electropolished to remove the surface oxide and the heat-affected edge left by laser cutting. The seat ring wall thickness after electropolishing lies within the range stated for the seat ring wall thickness, and in some embodiments the seat ring wall thickness is checked after electropolishing, because electropolishing removes material from the whole surface including the root of each convolution of the convoluted wall (32). In other embodiments the seat ring is finished by mechanical or chemical polishing in place of electropolishing. In some embodiments the depth of electropolishing is limited at the inner surface of the seat ring so as to preserve a defined wiping edge, formed by the laser-cut profile, that is presented to the poppet (40) during the snap-back wipe; electropolishing rounds edges, and a limited depth of electropolishing retains the sharper edge while still removing the oxide.

#### Alternative forming routes

The laser-cutting, shape-setting and electropolishing route is the route by which the seat rings (30) of Example 1 were made. The seat ring is not limited to that route, and any route that yields a deflectable ring element having the sealing edge (34), the convoluted wall (32) or another axial-to-radial conversion geometry, and the at-rest geometry described herein may be used. In some embodiments the convoluted wall of a seat ring of superelastic alloy is formed by roll-forming with profiled rollers, in stages, with shape-setting between the stages. In some embodiments the convoluted wall is formed by internal pressure in a split die; this route is more readily applied to a seat ring of stainless steel than to one of superelastic alloy at room temperature. In embodiments in which the seat ring is formed from a resilient polymer, such as PEEK, the seat ring may be injection moulded, and the shape-setting and electropolishing steps described above do not apply. In embodiments having a fixed seat (130) and a separate wiper ring (132), the wiper ring may be stamped or deep drawn from strip, as may a deflectable ring element in the form of a conical annular disc. A slotted wall (332) or a cellular wall (432) may be produced by laser cutting from tube stock as described above, or by photochemical etching of flat sheet followed by rolling and joining; the joined seam is a site of reduced fatigue life in a superelastic alloy, and for that material laser cutting from tube stock is preferred. None of the alternative forming routes of this section has been practised for the valve core described herein; they are described so that the seat ring is not limited to the route by which the prototype seat rings were made.

#### Assembly of the valve core

The core body (20) and the poppet (40) of the illustrated embodiment are turned from bar by ordinary machining, with no special process. As shown in FIG. 8C, the seat ring (30) is pressed into the core body bore (22) until it seats against the internal shoulder (24), with an interference fit of approximately 0.03 mm on diameter. The interference fit retains the seat ring axially against the internal shoulder and rotationally in the core body. As described above in connection with the closed state, in some embodiments the interference fit is confined to an axial band of the seat ring adjacent the internal shoulder, the remainder of the outer surface of the seat ring being free of the core body bore.

In other embodiments the seat ring (30) is retained in the core body (20) by ring retention other than, or in addition to, the interference fit. As shown in FIG. 8D, a crimped lip (224) of the core body may be rolled or swaged over the end of the seat ring remote from the internal shoulder (24). As shown in FIG. 8E, a retaining sleeve (324) pressed or threaded into the core body may clamp the seat ring against the internal shoulder. Each of these frees the outer surface of the seat ring from radial interference along its length. Further ring retention species, and their combination with the interference fit as supplementary retention, are described above in connection with the alternatives. In some embodiments the assembly step includes a check of the press-in force, or of the press-in travel, of the seat ring, so that a seat ring pressed with an interference at the tight end of the manufacturing tolerance is detected before it is assembled into a valve core in which the interference could suppress the radial deflection of the seat ring.

With the seat ring (30) retained, the poppet (40) is inserted so that its poppet stem (41) is guided by the core body (20) and its sealing face (42) rests on the sealing edge (34) of the seat ring, the return spring (50) is fitted so that its first spring stage (52) biases the poppet towards the closed state, and the lock nut (70) is fitted. The assembled valve core is threaded into the stem bore (12) of the valve stem (10). Where the valve core is supplied as a replacement valve core, this last step is performed by the user on an existing valve stem. Where the seat ring, or the seat ring with the poppet and the return spring, is supplied as a kit, the remaining assembly steps are performed by the recipient.

#### Examples

Example 1 and Comparative Example 1 report a bench test that was actually performed; every value in them is a measured or as-used value from that test. Examples 2, 3 and 4 are prophetic examples: they describe embodiments that have been conceived but not built or tested, they are written in the present or conditional tense, and no result is reported for them.

#### Example 1 (actual): superelastic seat ring embodiment

Three valve cores, designated P-1, P-2 and P-3, were built to the illustrated embodiment. Each comprised a core body (20) and valve stem (10) of free-machining brass CW614N, the valve stem being a Presta-pattern valve stem of 6 mm outer diameter; a seat ring (30) of superelastic alloy, NiTi of nominally 55.8 wt% nickel content, specified to have an austenite finish temperature below 5 °C, with a convoluted wall (32) and a sealing edge (34); a poppet (40) of brass with a conical sealing face (42) seating metal to metal on the sealing edge, and a purge shoulder (44) on the chamber side of the sealing face; a return spring (50) of stainless steel music wire having a first spring stage (52) and a second spring stage (54); a purge gap (60) closed in the closed state; and a lock nut (70).

The seat rings (30) of Example 1 had a seat ring inner diameter of 2.3 mm, a seat ring wall thickness of 0.20 mm, a seat ring free length of 3.0 mm and a convolution amplitude of 0.12 mm, and were made from tube stock of 2.7 mm outer diameter and 0.20 mm wall by the laser-cutting, shape-setting and electropolishing route described above, and were pressed into the core body (20) against the internal shoulder (24) with an interference fit of approximately 0.03 mm on diameter. The valve cores of Example 1 had a crack pressure of 1.0 bar, a normal stroke of 0.6 mm, an over-travel threshold of 3.5 bar, an over-travel distance of 0.5 mm and a purge gap opening of 0.15 mm radial. The crack pressure, normal stroke, over-travel threshold, over-travel distance and purge gap opening are the design values of the prototype valve cores; the over-travel distance and the purge gap opening were not measured during the bench test.

The bench test was run from 12 August 2026 to 20 August 2026. Each valve core of Example 1, and each conventional valve core of Comparative Example 1, was fitted to an identical inflatable chamber, a 700×28 bicycle inner tube, charged with 60 ml of liquid sealant, a latex emulsion containing ammonia and suspended particles. Each inflation cycle consisted of an inflation event in which the inflatable chamber was inflated from fully deflated to 6 bar with the same inflation device, a floor pump, by the same operator; a hold of 24 hours at 22 °C; and full deflation through the inflation valve. The inflation time was measured from coupling of the inflation device to the supply side until the inflatable chamber reached 6 bar. Leak-down was measured over each 24-hour hold. A valve core was recorded as failed at the first inflation cycle at which its leak-down exceeded 0.5 bar in 24 hours, or at which the inflation device failed to open the valve within 5 seconds of engagement, the latter being recorded as blockage.

Each of the valve cores P-1, P-2 and P-3 completed 40 inflation cycles without failure, at which point the test was stopped. The inflation time of P-1 was 43 s at inflation cycle 1 and 47 s at inflation cycle 40; that of P-2 was 41 s at inflation cycle 1 and 46 s at inflation cycle 40; and that of P-3 was 44 s at inflation cycle 1 and 50 s at inflation cycle 40. The leak-down at inflation cycle 40 was 0.1 bar in 24 hours for P-1, 0.1 bar in 24 hours for P-2 and 0.2 bar in 24 hours for P-3. On every inflation event of every valve core of Example 1, an audible snap was heard on removal of the inflation device. The audible snap indicates that a shape recovery event of the seat ring (30) occurred on removal of the inflation device at these test conditions; it is not a measurement of the over-travel distance, of the purge gap opening, or of the extent of the snap-back wipe.

After inflation cycle 40 the valve cores P-1, P-2 and P-3 were removed and inspected under a stereo microscope. In each, the sealing edge (34) of the seat ring (30) and the sealing face (42) of the poppet (40) were clean of sealant film. Cured sealant film was present on the outer surface of the seat ring in the region of the purge gap (60). The bench test did not include fatigue testing of the seat ring beyond the 40 inflation cycles, did not include testing at temperatures other than 22 °C, did not include cold-shock testing such as would occur with a compressed-CO2 inflation device, and did not separate the contribution of the air purge from that of the snap-back wipe.

#### Comparative Example 1 (actual): conventional valve core

Three conventional valve cores, designated C-1, C-2 and C-3, all commercial Presta-pattern valve cores of one manufacturer's standard type and lacking a deflectable ring element and a purge gap, were tested under the protocol of Example 1 on identical inflatable chambers with the same liquid sealant. C-1 failed by blockage at inflation cycle 14, its inflation time having risen from 41 s at inflation cycle 1 to 68 s at inflation cycle 13. C-2 failed by leak-down of 0.7 bar in 24 hours at inflation cycle 12, its inflation time having risen from 40 s at inflation cycle 1 to 44 s at inflation cycle 11. C-3 failed by blockage at inflation cycle 18, its inflation time having risen from 42 s at inflation cycle 1 to 75 s at inflation cycle 17. The conventional valve cores thus exhibited both of the failure modes described in the background, leak-down and blockage, between inflation cycles 12 and 18.

#### Example 2 (prophetic): resilient polymer seat ring embodiment

In this prophetic example the valve core of Example 1 is built with a seat ring (30) formed from a resilient polymer, PEEK, in place of the superelastic alloy, the seat ring having the same convoluted wall (32) and sealing edge (34), and the core body (20), poppet (40) with sealing face (42) and purge shoulder (44), return spring (50), purge gap (60) and lock nut (70) being as in Example 1. The seat ring of this example would be made by a route suited to the polymer, such as injection moulding, without the shape-setting and electropolishing steps of Example 1. On over-travel the purge shoulder would deflect the seat ring radially and open the purge gap, so that the air purge would operate as in Example 1. On removal of the inflation device the shape recovery of the polymer seat ring would be slower, and its recovery force lower, than the superelastic recovery of the seat ring of Example 1, so that the snap-back wipe would be weaker; the air purge would provide cleaning action independently of the snap-back wipe. The dimensions and the pressure values of Example 1 correspond to the stiffness and recovery characteristics of the superelastic alloy, and the person skilled in the art would adapt them to those of the resilient polymer. No inflation cycle, leak-down or inflation time result exists for this example.

#### Example 3 (prophetic): fixed seat and wiper ring embodiment

In this prophetic example, shown in FIG. 10A and FIG. 10B, the valve core comprises a core body (20) and valve stem (10) as in Example 1, a rigid fixed seat (130) of brass on which the sealing face (42) of the poppet (40) seats in the closed state, and a separate wiper ring (132) of stainless spring steel positioned on the chamber side of the fixed seat. The poppet, return spring (50) and lock nut (70) are as in Example 1. At and above the crack pressure the poppet would lift its sealing face from the fixed seat by the normal stroke, with the purge gap (60) closed. Above the over-travel threshold the purge shoulder (44) would deflect the wiper ring radially outward, opening the purge gap between the outer surface of the wiper ring and the core body bore (22), and the air purge would flow along the purge flow path (62) into the inflatable chamber. On removal of the inflation device the wiper ring would undergo shape recovery and the purge gap would close. This embodiment separates the sealing function, performed by the fixed seat, from the purging function and any wiping function, performed by the wiper ring, at the cost of one more part and greater axial length than Example 1. No result exists for this example.

#### Example 4 (prophetic): corrosion-resistant core body and wear-resistant sealing face

In this prophetic example the valve core of Example 1, Example 2 or Example 3 is built with a core body (20) of stainless steel 303 in place of brass CW614N, for corrosion resistance in service with a liquid sealant containing ammonia. A core body of stainless steel 303 has been machined; the bench test of Example 1 was performed with core bodies of brass only, and no result exists for a valve core having a core body of stainless steel 303. Independently of the core body material, in this prophetic example the poppet (40) carries a sealing face (42) of hard-anodised aluminium in place of the brass sealing face of Example 1, as a wear-resistant sealing face for repeated metal-to-metal seating on the sealing edge (34) of the seat ring (30) or on the fixed seat (130). The hard-anodised aluminium sealing face has been considered but not built. No result exists for either variant.

#### Robustness and failure modes

The following paragraphs describe conditions under which the performance of the valve core may be degraded, and the features described herein that address them. Where a feature has been tested it is so stated; where a feature has not been tested, it is described as a feature that may be used and no result is asserted for it.

Sealant film was found after 40 inflation cycles on the outer surface of the seat ring (30) in the region of the purge gap (60), a region that is closed, and therefore not purged, whenever the valve core is not in the purge state. Whether that film accumulates further over a service life longer than 40 inflation cycles, and whether it affects the opening or closing of the purge gap, was not tested. In some embodiments the purge gap opening is selected towards the upper end of the range of 0.10 mm to 0.25 mm, so as to increase the clearance and the velocity of the air purge in that region. In some embodiments the surface finish applied to the seat ring, such as electropolishing, is also applied to the adjoining surface of the core body bore (22) so as to reduce the adhesion of sealant film to it. The snap-back wipe occurring on every shape recovery may itself prevent sealant film in the closed purge gap from hardening; this has not been tested beyond 40 inflation cycles.

The seat ring (30) is subjected to radial deflection and shape recovery on every purge state, and the strain is concentrated at the root of each convolution of the convoluted wall (32). The fatigue life of the seat ring beyond the 40 inflation cycles of Example 1 has not been tested. In some embodiments the convoluted wall has two convolutions rather than one, so that the strain of a given radial deflection is distributed between them; in some embodiments a fillet radius is provided at the root of each convolution. No fatigue result is asserted for either feature.

The seat ring (30) of the illustrated embodiment is specified to have an austenite finish temperature below 5 °C, so that it is superelastic across ordinary riding temperatures. Its martensite start temperature has not been measured, and its behaviour if chilled below the martensite start temperature, whether by cold weather or by the expansion of gas from a compressed-CO2 inflation device while the seat ring is in the purge state, has not been tested; superelastic shape recovery may be impaired below the martensite start temperature until the seat ring warms. In some embodiments the superelastic alloy is selected, by its nickel content or by its shape-setting, to have a lower austenite finish temperature, giving more margin against chilling. In other embodiments the deflectable ring element is formed from a resilient metal or a resilient polymer whose shape recovery does not depend on a phase transformation, as described above in connection with the materials of the deflectable ring element, or the shape recovery of the deflectable ring element is positively driven by a formation of the poppet (40) on its return, as also described above. No value of the martensite start temperature and no cold-inflation margin is asserted herein.

Retention of the seat ring (30) in the illustrated embodiment relies on the interference fit of approximately 0.03 mm on diameter against the internal shoulder (24). The working range of the interference has not been determined, and no test of retention under thermal cycling or vibration has been performed. In some embodiments, supplementary ring retention is provided in addition to the interference fit, such as the crimped lip (224), the retaining sleeve (324), an adhesive bond, staking or laser welding described herein, so that the seat ring cannot migrate axially, rotate or come free into the inflatable chamber. In some embodiments the interference is held within a specified tolerance band and the press-in force or travel is checked at assembly, as described above, so that an interference at the tight end of the tolerance does not suppress the radial deflection of the seat ring or crack it at assembly, and an interference at the loose end does not leave the seat ring unretained.

In the illustrated embodiment the seal in the closed state is formed by metal-to-metal contact between the brass sealing face (42) of the poppet (40) and the sealing edge (34) of the seat ring (30). The bench test of Example 1 ran for 40 inflation cycles; the wear of the sealing face and the sealing edge over a service life of several thousand inflation cycles has not been tested. In some embodiments the sealing face is a hard-anodised aluminium sealing face, or carries a wear-resistant finish, as described in Example 4 and in connection with the alternatives above.

If the pressure differential rises rapidly and well beyond the over-travel threshold, for example on inflation from a shop compressor, the poppet (40) is driven into over-travel with greater force than on inflation with a floor pump. In some embodiments over-travel is limited independently of the deflectable ring element, as described above in connection with over-travel, so that the radial deflection of the seat ring (30) cannot exceed the recoverable strain of its material whatever the pressure differential applied. The bench test of Example 1 applied one inflation event per 24-hour inflation cycle with a floor pump; rapid repeated inflation events in a short period, inflation from a compressor, and the first inflation event after prolonged storage of the inflatable chamber in a deflated state, in which the sealant film near the inflation valve may have cured more fully than between the daily inflation cycles of the test, were not tested.

The austenite finish temperature, the convolution amplitude, the seat ring wall thickness after electropolishing and the over-travel threshold set by the second spring stage (54) each depend on a manufacturing step for which Example 1 gives the values used for the prototype valve cores and not a process window. In some embodiments production seat rings (30) are verified by sampling the austenite finish temperature after shape-setting, by measuring the convolution amplitude before shape-setting and the seat ring wall thickness after electropolishing, and by sorting or verifying the rate of the second spring stage at assembly, so that each production valve core lies within the ranges stated herein. These verification steps are described as manufacturing options and have not been practised for the prototype valve cores.

The liquid sealant of Example 1 contained ammonia. Brass is subject to stress-corrosion cracking in ammoniacal environments as a matter of general materials knowledge, and for that reason a core body (20) of stainless steel 303 is described in Example 4 and in connection with the alternatives above. Whether stress-corrosion attack occurs in service on the core body of brass CW614N or on the poppet (40) of the illustrated embodiment has not been tested. Liquid sealants that are free of ammonia, and liquid sealants of higher viscosity or with abrasive particulate fillers, were not tested, and the effect of the valve core with them is not asserted herein.

For storage and transport the lock nut (70) is used to hold the poppet (40) in the closed state, in which the purge shoulder (44) is clear of the seat ring (30) and the seat ring is at rest. A valve cap or accessory having an internal pin that depresses the poppet should not be used in a manner that holds the poppet in over-travel, since that would hold the seat ring in sustained radial deflection rather than the transient radial deflection of the purge state. Because the seat ring wall thickness is between 0.15 mm and 0.30 mm, a tool inserted into the valve core in the manner used to clear a conventional valve core may damage the convoluted wall (32); the valve core described herein is intended to be cleaned by the air purge and the snap-back wipe without tool insertion.

#### Industrial applicability and use

The inflation valve and the valve core described herein are for use with an inflatable chamber that holds liquid sealant, including a bicycle inner tube, as tested in Example 1, and a tubeless bicycle tyre set-up, in which the liquid sealant is likewise injected through the valve stem (10) and drawn towards the valve core on each inflation event. Use in a tubeless bicycle tyre set-up has not been tested. The valve core opens, closes and enters the purge state in response to the pressure differential alone; no tool and no user action beyond coupling and removing the inflation device is required for either cleaning action.

The purge state is reached when the pressure differential applied by the inflation device exceeds the over-travel threshold, which in Example 1 was 3.5 bar and was exceeded on every inflation event with a floor pump inflating the inflatable chamber from fully deflated to 6 bar. Whether a given inflation device reaches the over-travel threshold depends on the pressure differential it applies at the supply side: it is expected that floor pumps of the kind used in Example 1 do so, and hand pumps, compressors and compressed-CO2 inflation devices may or may not do so depending on the pressure differential they generate; only the floor pump of Example 1 has been tested. An inflation event in which the pressure differential does not exceed the over-travel threshold inflates the inflatable chamber through the main passage (64) in the normal-open state without entering the purge state, the purge gap (60) remaining closed; the air purge and the snap-back wipe occur on the inflation events in which the over-travel threshold is exceeded. Any inflation gas may serve as the air purge.

The valve core may be supplied as original equipment fitted to the valve stem (10) of an inflation valve, or as a replacement valve core for fitting to an existing valve stem in place of a conventional valve core, in which case it is threaded into the stem bore (12) in the same manner as the conventional valve core it replaces, and the lock nut (70) of the existing inflation valve may be retained. As shown in FIG. 5, the seat ring (30), or the seat ring together with the poppet (40) and the return spring (50), may also be supplied as a kit for assembly into a core body (20), as described above in connection with component supply. The inflation valve may be supplied fitted to a bicycle inner tube, a tubeless bicycle tyre set-up, a wheel, or a bicycle.
